# Strategic Frameworks

Mental models for game-level decision making.

## Who's the Beatdown

### Mike Flores's Foundational Insight

In every game of Magic, one player is the **beatdown** (aggressor) and one is the **control** (defender). These roles are not chosen—they're determined by deck matchups and game state.

**"Misassignment of Role = Game Loss"**

If you play beatdown when you should be control, you'll overextend into removal and run out of threats. If you play control when you should be beatdown, you'll give opponents time to execute their superior late game.

### Determining Your Role

Ask: **Who wins if the game goes long?**

The player with inevitability plays control. The other player must be beatdown.

**Factors determining inevitability:**
- Card advantage engines
- Superior late-game threats
- Combo potential
- Grinding capability

### Role in Matchups

**Same archetype, different builds:**
- Mono-Red vs Mono-Red: More reach = beatdown, more creatures = control
- Jund vs Jund: More planeswalkers = control, more discard = beatdown

**Cross-archetype:**
- Aggro vs Midrange: Aggro is beatdown
- Midrange vs Control: Midrange is beatdown
- Control vs Combo: Control is often beatdown (must pressure before combo)

### Role Shifts Mid-Game

Roles can change based on:
- Life total differential
- Board state
- Cards in hand
- Topdecks

Example: Control deck stumbles on mana → must become beatdown with whatever creatures it has before opponent recovers.

### Common Role Errors

**Playing control with beatdown deck:**
- Holding back attackers "to block"
- Saving burn for creatures instead of face
- Taking the long game against superior late game

**Playing beatdown with control deck:**
- Running threats into obvious removal
- Overcommitting to board before establishing control
- Ignoring card advantage for board presence

## Inevitability

### Zvi Mowshowitz's Extension

Inevitability is the concept of which player wins given infinite time. It's not about who's currently winning—it's about who WILL win if the game continues.

**Inevitability sources:**
- Card advantage engines (Phyrexian Arena, planeswalkers)
- Superior threats (Teferi, Hero of Dominaria vs Grizzly Bears)
- Combo potential (Splinter Twin combo pieces)
- Mana advantage (Wilderness Reclamation)
- Win conditions that don't require attacking

### Inevitability is Contextual

The same deck can have or lack inevitability depending on opponent:
- UW Control has inevitability vs Mono-Red (will stabilize eventually)
- UW Control lacks inevitability vs Nexus of Fate combo (they win infinite turns)

### Playing With Inevitability

When you have inevitability:
- Trade resources aggressively
- Don't take risks
- Extend the game
- Value your life total (need to survive)

### Playing Without Inevitability

When you lack inevitability:
- Must find a kill window
- Take calculated risks
- Force opponent to have answers
- Life total becomes a resource to spend

### Inevitability in Limited

Limited inevitability factors:
- Bomb rares
- Card draw
- Evasion density
- Recursive threats

The player with the better late game should slow the game down. The other must find ways through.

## The Fundamental Turn

### Zvi's Kill Clock Concept

The **fundamental turn** is when a deck "actually wins"—not when it technically deals 20 damage, but when victory becomes inevitable.

**Example fundamental turns:**
- Mono-Red Aggro: Turn 4-5 (must have lethal by here)
- Splinter Twin: Turn 4 (combo threatens to win)
- UW Control: Turn 8+ (stabilized and pulling ahead)
- Tron: Turn 3-4 (Karn or Wurmcoil deployed)

### Using Fundamental Turn for Evaluation

Cards are evaluated against fundamental turn:
- Does this help me reach my fundamental turn?
- Does this disrupt opponent's fundamental turn?
- Does this move my fundamental turn earlier?

**5-mana creature in aggro:** Too slow—game should be decided by then
**5-mana creature in midrange:** On-curve threat
**5-mana creature in control:** Too small for the late game

### Fundamental Turn in Matchups

Matchups are races between fundamental turns:
- If your fundamental turn is earlier, opponent must interact
- If opponent's fundamental turn is earlier, you must interact
- If they're similar, positioning and play skill dominate

### Sideboard Strategy and Fundamental Turn

Sideboarding often aims to:
- Delay opponent's fundamental turn (add removal, hate cards)
- Accelerate your fundamental turn (more threats, combo pieces)
- Change your fundamental turn entirely (transformative sideboard)

## Big Games vs Small Games

### Sam Black's Framework

Not all games are equal. Some are **big games** (high-impact, swingy) and some are **small games** (low-impact, grindy).

**Big games:** Large swings in advantage, explosive plays matter
**Small games:** Incremental advantages, efficiency matters

### The Elvish Visionary Example

**Elvish Visionary** (1G, 1/1, draw a card when ETB):
- Small game: Excellent. Replaces itself, develops board, trades eventually.
- Big game: Terrible. 1/1 body is meaningless when cards have huge impact.

**Elderfang Disciple** (1B, 1/1, opponent discards when ETB):
- Small game: Worse than Visionary. Their worst card vs your best card.
- Big game: Better than Visionary. Disrupting their bomb matters.

### Identifying Game Size

**Signs of big games:**
- Powerful bombs in format
- High variance cards
- Combo potential
- Games decided by specific cards

**Signs of small games:**
- Flat power level
- Games decided by board development
- Attrition matters
- Card quality relatively even

### Card Evaluation by Game Size

| Card Type | Small Game | Big Game |
|-----------|------------|----------|
| Efficient creatures | Premium | Medium |
| Card draw | Good | Depends |
| Removal | Good | Premium |
| Discard | Weak | Strong |
| Counterspells | Weak | Strong |
| Bombs | Good | Premium |

### Format Design Implications

Cube designers control game size through:
- Power level (higher = bigger games)
- Combo density (more combo = bigger games)
- Removal quality (better removal = smaller games)
- Threat density (more threats = smaller games)

## Framework Integration

### Using All Frameworks Together

When analyzing a game or format:

1. **Determine roles** (Who's the Beatdown)
2. **Identify inevitability** (Who wins the long game)
3. **Calculate fundamental turns** (When do decks "win")
4. **Assess game size** (Swingy or grindy format)

### Framework Application Example

**Modern Burn vs Jund:**
- Role: Burn is beatdown (Jund has card advantage inevitability)
- Inevitability: Jund has it (Burn runs out of cards)
- Fundamental turns: Burn T4-5, Jund T6-7
- Game size: Medium (Burn is linear, Jund has variance)

**Burn's strategy:** Must win before Jund stabilizes. Count to 20, minimize interaction.

**Jund's strategy:** Survive to turn 6. Trade resources, gain life, deploy threats that must be answered.

### When Frameworks Conflict

Sometimes frameworks suggest different plays:
- "Who's the Beatdown" says attack
- Resource evaluation says hold back

Resolution: Context determines priority. Life total, specific cards in hand, known information all factor in. Frameworks are guidelines, not rules.
