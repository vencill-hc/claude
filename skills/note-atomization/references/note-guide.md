## Note Types

### Concept Note
For ideas, principles, mental models:
- Define precisely in your own words
- Compare to related/contrasting concepts
- Give concrete examples
- Explain why it matters

### Claim Note
For assertions, findings, arguments:
- State the claim clearly
- Provide supporting evidence/reasoning
- Note counterarguments or limitations
- Connect to your existing beliefs

### Procedure Note
For processes, how-tos, techniques:
- When to use this procedure
- Key steps and decision points
- Common mistakes to avoid
- How to verify success

### Question Note
For open questions worth tracking:
- State the question precisely
- Why you care about the answer
- Current best guesses or leads
- Related questions

## Tag Conventions

### Topic tags (what it's about)
- `topic/learning`, `topic/programming`, `topic/claude-code`
- Nest for specificity: `topic/programming/python`

### Type tags (what kind of note)
- `type/concept` - Ideas, principles, mental models
- `type/claim` - Assertions, findings, arguments
- `type/procedure` - How-tos, processes
- `type/question` - Open questions worth tracking

### Status tags (note maturity)
- `status/seedling` - Initial capture, needs development
- `status/developing` - Work in progress
- `status/evergreen` - Fully developed, stable

## Anti-Patterns

### The Summary Trap
Don't create notes that summarize sources. Create notes about IDEAS that happen to come from sources.

**Bad:** "Chapter 3 Summary: The author discusses X, Y, and Z..."
**Good:** Three separate notes, each developing X, Y, or Z as standalone ideas

### The Quote Collection
Don't just collect quotes. Transform them into your own understanding.

**Bad:** A note that's mostly blockquotes
**Good:** Your synthesis, with quotes as supporting evidence

### The Topic Dump
Don't create catch-all notes for broad topics.

**Bad:** "Machine Learning" note with everything you know
**Good:** Dozens of atomic notes, densely interlinked

### The Orphan Note
Every note should connect to others. If it stands alone, either:
- It's too specific/trivial to keep
- You haven't found the connections yet (keep exploring)

# Most note-taking fails because it treats notes as storage

The dominant mental model of note-taking is "capture and store"—treating notes like a filing cabinet where information goes to be retrieved later. This fundamentally misunderstands what makes notes valuable.

Notes aren't valuable because they store information (we have books and the internet for that). They're valuable when they transform how we think. This is why [[Writing forces clearer thinking than passive reading|writing forces clearer thinking]]—the act of creating notes should develop understanding, not just record it.

This explains why most people's notes are useless: they optimize for capture fidelity rather than cognitive transformation. [[The slip-box succeeds by creating idea connections not storage|Luhmann's slip-box worked]] precisely because it prioritized connections over collection.

## Why This Matters

Every time I take notes, I should ask: "Am I storing or thinking?" If just storing, I'm wasting effort. [[Evergreen notes should be concept-oriented not source-oriented|Organizing by concept rather than source]] helps enforce this distinction.

# Writing forces clearer thinking than passive reading

Reading creates an illusion of understanding. You follow along, nod at the arguments, and feel like you "get it." But try to explain what you read, and the gaps become painfully obvious.

Writing forces these gaps into the open. You can't write clearly about something you don't understand clearly. The struggle to articulate is the struggle to think. This is why [[Notes should be in your own words to force understanding|notes must be in your own words]]—paraphrasing is where the cognitive work happens.

This insight explains why [[Most note-taking fails because it treats notes as storage|most note-taking fails]]. Copying quotes or highlighting passages feels productive but bypasses the hard work of synthesis. The reading without writing was always incomplete.

## Why This Matters

I should treat writing as my primary thinking tool, not as output that happens after thinking. Combined with [[Retrieval practice strengthens memory more than re-exposure|retrieval practice]], writing becomes both a thinking tool and a memory tool.

### Key Observations

Notice how each note:
- Has a title that's a complete claim/thought
- Develops ONE idea fully
- Weaves links naturally into sentences (not just in a list)
- Includes personal relevance ("Why This Matters")
- Could be understood without reading the original source
- Connects to other notes being created

The transformation extracts 5 bullet points into at least 5 interconnected notes, each standing as a complete thought.
